Introduction

Public procurement is a principal channel through which governments acquire artificial intelligence (AI) systems and services, and the market for such contracts is of direct interest to suppliers, buyers, and regulators (Hickok 2024). Quantifying this market requires deciding which procurement notices concern AI. In practice, this decision is usually operationalised with English keyword lists, as in studies that measure AI demand from online vacancies (Acemoglu et al. 2022; Babina et al. 2024) and in public trackers of AI contracts. Keyword measures are transparent and cheap, but their accuracy is rarely reported against a reference annotation.

Two features of procurement data make this omission consequential. First, European notices published in Tenders Electronic Daily (TED) are written in 24 languages (Ahmia et al. 2018); in the 55,508 IT and software award notices analysed here, only 6.2% were published in English. An English dictionary may therefore miss AI purchases in a language-dependent way, which distorts cross-country comparisons. Second, an AI indicator produced by a dictionary or a classifier is often used as a regressor in a second-stage analysis. Recent work shows that plugging machine-generated variables into regressions yields biased estimates and invalid confidence intervals unless the measurement error is addressed (Battaglia et al. 2025; Egami et al. 2023), and that correction methods differ in finite samples (Audinet de Pieuchon et al. 2025).

This paper examines how the measurement of AI-relatedness in procurement notices affects three quantities: classification accuracy, the estimated share of AI notices, and the estimated association between AI-relatedness and single bidding, a standard indicator of weak competition in procurement (Fazekas et al. 2016). We compare six scalable methods—two keyword dictionaries, a zero-shot natural language inference (NLI) classifier, an embedding-prototype score, and two supervised classifiers—against a codebook-based reference annotation of a stratified probability sample, and we apply design-based supervised learning (DSL) (Egami et al. 2023) to obtain prevalence and regression estimates that account for classifier error. All hypotheses, the sample design, and the codebook were fixed before annotation in a pre-specified analysis plan.

The contributions are threefold. (i) We provide, to our knowledge, the first language-stratified accuracy assessment of AI identification in multilingual procurement notices. (ii) We show how prevalence and competition estimates change with the measurement method and after DSL correction. (iii) We release code, prompts, and notice-level derived data so that the procedure can be reused for procurement monitoring. The contribution is methodological and empirical; no new learning algorithm is proposed.

Related Work

Text-based measurement of AI demand.

Labour-economics studies identify AI demand through AI-related terms in job postings (Acemoglu et al. 2022; Babina et al. 2024), and large language models (LLMs) have been used to score occupational exposure to AI (Eloundou et al. 2024). Text-as-data surveys emphasise that dictionary and supervised measures trade transparency against recall and that their error should be quantified (Gentzkow et al. 2019). These studies mainly analyse English text and seldom report precision and recall against an independent annotation.

Procurement analytics.

TED data support corpus construction (Ahmia et al. 2018), national award databases (Potin et al. 2023), process mining (Nai et al. 2023), and CPV code assignment (Siciliani et al. 2023). Single bidding and related indicators are widely used to assess competition and integrity risk (Fazekas et al. 2016; Decarolis and Giorgiantonio 2022), while the number of bidders has proven difficult to predict from notice features (Loza Mencía et al. 2013). To our knowledge, no study has evaluated how AI contracts are identified in multilingual TED notices.

LLM annotation and inference with predicted variables.

LLMs can match or exceed crowd workers on several annotation tasks (Gilardi et al. 2023; Törnberg 2023), but performance varies widely across tasks (Ziems et al. 2024). Prediction-powered inference (PPI) (Angelopoulos et al. 2023) and DSL (Egami et al. 2023) combine a small labelled sample with abundant predictions to obtain valid estimates; benchmark evidence suggests that DSL often reduces bias more than PPI, although not uniformly (Audinet de Pieuchon et al. 2025). Multilingual sentence encoders (Wang et al. 2024; Reimers and Gurevych 2020) and NLI-based zero-shot classification (Yin et al. 2019; He et al. 2023) offer language-independent alternatives to dictionaries, with performance that varies by task and language (Muennighoff et al. 2023; Conneau et al. 2020).

Method

Problem Formulation

Let i = 1, …, N index award notices in the analysis frame and let X_(i) ∈ {0, 1} denote AI-relatedness as defined by a fixed codebook. X_(i) is observed only for a labelled subsample ℒ drawn with known inclusion probabilities π_(i); for every notice, a method m produces a prediction X̂_(i)^(m) or a score. We target (a) the precision, recall, and F1 of each method relative to X, (b) the prevalence θ = N⁻¹∑_(i)X_(i), and (c) the coefficient β on X_(i) in a model of single bidding Y_(i) with controls W_(i). The reference annotation defines the construct operationally; it is not treated as expert ground truth.

Construct and Reference Annotation

A notice is AI-related if the procured object includes the development, supply, licensing, operation, or consulting and training on a system whose core functionality relies on AI techniques (machine learning, deep learning, natural language processing or LLMs, chatbots, computer vision, speech recognition or synthesis, or predictive models learned from data). Generic software, workflow automation without AI, dashboards without learning components, and hardware are coded as not AI-related. This definition follows the task-based view of AI used in vacancy studies (Acemoglu et al. 2022) and the procurement-risk framing of AI systems (Hickok 2024). Each sampled notice was annotated by a primary LLM (Claude Haiku 5.5) and a secondary LLM (Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite) with the same zero-shot prompt (temperature 0, JSON output); the primary label is the reference, and agreement is reported as Cohen’s κ. LLM annotation is used because it has shown accuracy comparable to or above crowd workers on several tasks (Gilardi et al. 2023), while its task dependence (Ziems et al. 2024) motivates the two-annotator check and the sensitivity analyses in Sect. 5.5.

Measurement Methods

M1 applies an English AI dictionary (22 patterns, e.g., artificial intelligence, machine learning, chatbot, LLM) to every notice, mirroring common practice. M2 adds core equivalents of artificial intelligence, machine learning, neural network, language model, and chatbot in the EU languages and the abbreviations KI and IA. M3 is zero-shot NLI with mDeBERTa-v3-base-mnli-xnli (He et al. 2023; Yin et al. 2019) and the hypothesis “This procurement is about artificial intelligence” (entailment probability  ≥ 0.5). M4 is the maximum cosine similarity between a notice and five English prototype queries in the multilingual-e5-small space (Wang et al. 2024), thresholded within training folds. M5, the proposed measurement, is an L₂-regularised logistic regression on multilingual-e5-small embeddings trained on the reference labels with weights 1/π_(i). M6 replaces the embeddings with character 3–5-gram TF-IDF features, a strong classical baseline for short multilingual text. M5 and M6 represent the supervised route recommended when labels are available (Gentzkow et al. 2019); M3 and M4 require no labels. All label-trained components are cross-fitted: predictions for labelled notices come from five folds that exclude them, which prevents optimistic evaluation and is required for DSL (Egami et al. 2023).

Sampling Design and Design-Based Estimation

Because AI notices are rare, a simple random sample would contain few positives. The frame was partitioned into four strata: A (M1 hit), B (M2 hit but not M1), C (neither, but M4 similarity in the top 3%), and D (remaining notices), and simple random samples were drawn within strata (seed 2027). Accuracy metrics weight each labelled notice by 1/π_(i), which makes them estimates for the whole frame. For prevalence and regression, DSL replaces the unobserved X_(i) with the pseudo-outcome
$$\begin{equation}
\tilde{X}_i=\hat{g}(T_i)+\frac{R_i}{\pi_i}\bigl(X_i-\hat{g}(T_i)\bigr),
\end{equation}$$
where ĝ is the cross-fitted M5 probability, T_(i) the text, and R_(i) the labelling indicator. Because E[X̃_(i) ∣ T_(i)] = E[X_(i) ∣ T_(i)] under the known design, θ̂_(DSL) = N⁻¹∑_(i)X̃_(i) is unbiased whatever the classifier error. For the linear probability model Y_(i) = βX_(i) + W_(i)^(⊤)γ + ε_(i), we substitute X̃_(i) for X_(i) and for X_(i)² in the ordinary least squares moment conditions (valid because X_(i) is binary) and use sandwich standard errors. We use a linear probability model because the specification contains over 100 fixed effects, for which logit estimates are less stable, and report logit plug-in estimates as a robustness check.

Experimental Setup

Data

We retrieved all TED contract award notices (eForms type can-standard) published between 1 January 2024 and 31 December 2025 whose CPV classification falls under IT services (72000000) or software packages (48000000), using the public TED Search API v3. TED notices may be reused for commercial and non-commercial purposes under the reuse policy of the European Commission. After deduplication, 55,508 notices from 50 buyer countries in 23 languages remained; excluding notices with fewer than ten characters of title and description left an analysis frame of N = 54, 160, of which 3,308 (6.1%) were in English. The text of a notice is its procedure title and description in its first official language (median 378 characters, truncated at 2,000). No personal data are used, and notice texts are not redistributed.

The regression sample contains frame notices that report exactly one count of received tenders between 1 and 100, which excludes multi-lot notices with lot-level counts and implausible values flagged in prior TED work (Potin et al. 2023); it comprises 30,388 notices, 46.2% of which received a single tender. Controls are fixed effects for buyer country (absorbing institutional differences and the currency of the contract value), CPV division (market structure), procedure type, buyer legal type, and publication quarter, plus log contract value with a missing-value indicator; these controls follow standard procurement-competition specifications (Fazekas et al. 2016; Decarolis and Giorgiantonio 2022).

Pre-specified Hypotheses

The analysis plan (fixed before annotation and committed to the project repository) stated: H1 the recall of M1 is lower for non-English than for English notices; H2 M5 attains higher F1 than M1 (H2a) and M2 (H2b); H3 the DSL prevalence exceeds the M1 prevalence; and H4 the DSL coefficient on AI-relatedness in the single-bidding model differs from zero (two-sided). One amendment, recorded before the affected results were computed, restricted M3 to the labelled sample because its projected CPU time for the frame exceeded the plan’s six-hour rule. Analyses added after inspecting results are labelled post hoc.

Results

Reference Annotation

The two annotators agreed on 96.9% of the 1,113 notices with both labels (unweighted κ = 0.92). Weighted to the frame, κ was 0.73, and it was lower for English (0.48, n = 232) than for non-English notices (0.78, n = 881). Disagreement was concentrated in the unflagged strata: the four stratum-D notices coded AI-related by the primary annotator (e.g., a train-running forecasting module, biometric face detection support, and a genetics-analysis software licence) were all coded not AI-related, or left unparsed, by the secondary annotator. Because each stratum-D notice carries a weight of about 115, these four labels account for 460 of the 1,114 estimated AI notices (41%).

Classification Accuracy (H1, H2)

Table 2 reports design-weighted accuracy. The multilingual dictionary (M2) achieved the highest F1 (0.63, 95% CI 0.50–0.78), doubling the recall of the English dictionary (0.56 vs. 0.28) at similar precision (0.71 vs. 0.69). H1 was not supported under the primary reference: the recall of M1 was 0.25 for English and 0.28 for non-English notices (difference 0.04, CI −0.55 to 0.27), largely because one English stratum-D positive carries a large weight. H2 was not supported, and the observed difference had the opposite sign: the pre-specified design-weighted classifiers M5 and M6 predicted almost no positives at the 0.5 threshold (recall 0.03 and 0.05; F1 0.06 and 0.10), although M5 had the highest threshold-free AUPRC (0.33). The F1 differences M5−M1 (−0.34, CI −0.45 to −0.26) and M5−M2 (−0.57, CI −0.70 to −0.45) were negative (Holm-adjusted p = 1.00 for both one-sided tests).

Prevalence (H3)

The design-based estimates of the share of AI-related notices were 2.06% (Horvitz–Thompson, labels only; CI 1.38–2.94%) and 2.32% (DSL; CI 1.56–3.21%). Naive shares were 0.83% for M1, 1.62% for M2, 6.51% for M4, and below 0.2% for M5 and M6. The DSL estimate exceeded the M1 share by 1.49 percentage points (CI 0.73–2.38; Holm-adjusted p = 0.002), supporting H3: the English dictionary captures roughly one third of the estimated AI notices.

AI-Relatedness and Single Bidding (H4)

Table 3 reports the linear probability coefficients. With naive plug-in indicators, the multilingual dictionary suggested that AI-related contracts were 4.9 percentage points less likely to receive a single tender (CI −8.9 to −0.8), whereas the English dictionary (−1.7, CI −7.3 to 3.9) and the other methods gave estimates whose CIs included zero. The labelled-only estimate was 3.1 points (CI −8.0 to 14.1; n = 630). The pre-specified DSL estimate was uninformative (β̂ = −1.24, SE 17.99): the near-zero M5 probabilities for the four heavily weighted stratum-D positives produce extreme pseudo-outcomes. H4 therefore could not be assessed with useful precision.

Robustness and Post hoc Analyses

Results depended on the treatment of contested labels. When the reference required agreement of both annotators (X_(∧)), stratum D contained no positives, the estimated prevalence fell to 1.41% (DSL; CI 1.10–1.91%), the recall of M1 was lower for non-English than for English notices (difference −0.32, CI −0.61 to −0.06), and the DSL coefficient became estimable (5.7 points, CI −7.0 to 18.4). With the union reference (X_(∨)), results matched the primary analysis. Replacing the encoder with paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM raised M5’s F1 to 0.30 but left M2 the best method. Restricting H2 to non-English notices gave F1 values of 0.40 (M1), 0.66 (M2), and 0.05 (M5). A logit plug-in model reproduced the sign pattern of Table 3 (M2 odds ratio 0.77, SE of log-odds 0.11). Post hoc, training M5 without design weights and tuning its threshold within folds raised F1 to 0.35, still below M2 (difference −0.28, CI −0.39 to −0.18); using these probabilities in DSL gave a prevalence of 1.31% (CI 0.37–2.36%) and a coefficient of −24.9 points with a CI spanning −103 to 53.

Computational Cost

On the shared CPU, the dictionaries processed the frame in 7 s (M1) and 20 s (M2), and multilingual-e5-small embedding took 1,229 s (about 23 ms per notice). Zero-shot NLI was the slowest component (about 4 s per notice with four threads), which is why it was restricted to the labelled sample. Reference annotation of 1,200 notices by two LLMs cost US$0.14.

Discussion

Measurement.

The confirmatory result that survives multiplicity correction is H3: an English keyword list, the most common operationalisation, identified about one third of the AI-related notices estimated from the design-based sample. Adding core terms in the EU languages doubled recall without loss of precision, so most of the gain came from handling language rather than from a more complex model. This is consistent with the multilingual composition of TED (Ahmia et al. 2018) and with the transparency–recall trade-off discussed for dictionary measures (Gentzkow et al. 2019). The language-specific gap predicted by H1 appeared only when contested labels were removed; under the primary reference it was masked by the uncertainty from a few heavily weighted positives.

Why the supervised classifiers failed.

Contrary to H2, the embedding and TF-IDF classifiers underperformed the multilingual dictionary. Two features of the design explain this post hoc. First, training with inverse-probability weights gave the 450 stratum-D notices weights about 200 times larger than the dictionary strata, which pushed predicted probabilities towards zero; unweighted training raised F1 from 0.06 to 0.35. Second, with 364 positives, most of which contain explicit AI terms, a linear classifier on general-purpose sentence embeddings learned little beyond the dictionary signal. These results do not show that supervised measurement is ineffective in general; they show that, at this label budget, weighting choices that are appropriate for estimation can be harmful for training, an implementation risk that is easy to overlook.

Downstream inference.

The naive multilingual-dictionary regression produced a statistically significant negative association between AI-relatedness and single bidding, whereas design-based estimates were too imprecise to confirm or reject any association. The precision of DSL depends on how well ĝ predicts the rare positives in the low-probability stratum (Egami et al. 2023; Audinet de Pieuchon et al. 2025); here, four contested labels determined much of the variance. The practical lesson is that a significant coefficient obtained with a keyword indicator should not be read as evidence about AI contracts when the measurement error has not been quantified (Battaglia et al. 2025). For procurement monitoring, the results favour a multilingual dictionary for screening, combined with a labelled probability sample that devotes more of the budget to the unflagged mass, where most of the uncertainty lies.

Limitations

The reference labels come from an LLM codebook annotation rather than from expert coders, and agreement was only moderate for English notices; the construct is therefore “AI-related according to this codebook and annotator”, and the four contested stratum-D positives show that conclusions about prevalence depend on this choice. The frame covers only notices in two CPV divisions published in TED, i.e., mostly above EU thresholds, so AI purchases classified elsewhere or below thresholds are not observed. The regression excludes multi-lot notices and notices without a valid tender count, which may introduce selection, and the controls omit buyer experience and supplier history, so omitted-variable bias is possible; all associations are non-causal. The dictionaries were written by the authors and may contain translation errors, and the stratification used M1, M2, and M4 scores, which makes the precision of estimates for other methods dependent on this design. Finally, a single small encoder and linear classifiers were tested; fine-tuned or larger models may perform differently.

Conclusion

We evaluated how AI-related public procurement notices are identified in 54,160 multilingual TED award notices, using a pre-specified stratified reference sample and design-based estimation. A multilingual dictionary roughly doubled the recall of the common English keyword approach, the English approach captured about one third of the estimated AI notices, and an apparent negative association between AI-relatedness and single bidding under naive measurement could not be confirmed after accounting for measurement error. Future work should use expert-adjudicated labels, allocate more labels to the unflagged stratum, test fine-tuned multilingual classifiers, and extend the analysis to national systems such as the Korean KONEPS.
